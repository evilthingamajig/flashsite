About Malaika

About Malaika

Malaika was founded in 2007 in Kalebuka, a village of about 42,000 people near Lubumbashi in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In the Munama quarter where it works, access to basic services is extremely limited and only about 4% of women can read and write.

Rather than running a single program, Malaika built what it calls an ecosystem of empowerment: six pillars — a school, a community center, sports, vocational training, agriculture, and water and health — each designed to reinforce the others. The idea is that educating a girl is not enough on its own; her family and community need knowledge, opportunity and support for her to truly thrive.

It is working. Four cohorts of young women have graduated from the school, 99 alumni are now at university or working for local companies, some on scholarships abroad. A free school that removes this many barriers is a natural place for a study light that removes one more: the dark after sunset.

Their work

The ecosystem, in six pillars

Photo: Malaika

The Malaika School

Opened in 2011, it gives 430 girls a free primary and secondary education, with classes in French and English across math, science, information technology and the arts.

Photo: Malaika

Community Center

Built with FIFA in 2013, the center serves more than 6,000 youth and adults a year with free literacy and vocational classes, sports programs and internet access.

Photo: Malaika

Sports

Running through the school and community center, sports give students structured coaching, team play and competition — and give girls the same field as everyone else.

Photo: Malaika

Vocational Training

Free, locally-led courses in mechanics, plumbing, advanced IT and more, run with support from the Caterpillar Foundation. Since 2021, hundreds of young people have earned accredited certifications.

Photo: Malaika

Agriculture and Nutrition

An on-site farm grows organic fruit and vegetables and supplies two meals every school day for students and staff — more than 180,000 meals a year.

Photo: Malaika

Water and Health

34 wells now serve about 46,000 people in Kalebuka and surrounding villages, alongside more than 12,000 distributed mosquito nets, student health checks and hygiene campaigns.

The partnership

Our part

Flash Forward delivered 15 study lights to Malaika free of charge, adding an estimated 90 hours of study light every day. Malaika chooses which students receive each light: the partner knows the students and households of Kalebuka. Student volunteers in Bellevue build the lights; the partner puts them to work.

Each light costs $5.17 in parts, recharges in about 7 hours of sunlight and runs for up to about 12 hours on a full charge. For a student without electricity at home, that is the difference between studying and stopping at sunset.
