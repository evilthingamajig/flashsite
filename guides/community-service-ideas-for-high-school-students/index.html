Community service ideas for high school students: 28 that actually help

Good community service ideas for high school students solve a real need near you or online, fit your schedule, and come with someone who can verify your hours. Here are 28 concrete ideas, grouped by where you'd do them.

Want real experience now? Flash Forward's remote internship is open to high school and college students.

By the Flash Forward Foundation team · Updated October 12, 2026

How to pick a service idea that fits

The best community service ideas for high school students start with a need, not a number of hours. Ask who around you is short on help: a food bank, a younger student, a neighbor, a small nonprofit. Then pick something you'll keep doing for months. Steady service beats a one-day burst, for you and for the people you help.

Before you start, check what your school, club or honor society accepts. Some only count work for a nonprofit or public agency, and most need a supervisor's signature.

Service ideas at school

  1. Peer tutoring. Help classmates or younger students in a subject you're strong in, through a school tutoring program.
  2. Reading buddy at an elementary school. Many districts pair high schoolers with younger readers.
  3. New-student ambassador. Give tours, answer questions, and help new students find their footing.
  4. Library or media center helper. Shelve books, run displays, or help with tech.
  5. Organize a school supply drive. Collect backpacks and supplies for students who need them.
  6. Start or run a recycling or compost program. Work with custodial staff and a teacher sponsor.
  7. Help with events for families. Set up, translate, or run check-in at conferences and open houses.

Service ideas in your neighborhood

  1. Food bank or pantry shifts. Sort donations, pack boxes, or help with distribution days.
  2. Park and trail cleanups. Many local parks departments organize volunteer work days.
  3. Help at an animal shelter. Walking dogs, cleaning, or doing laundry all count.
  4. Visit or call older neighbors. Go through a senior center or community organization so it's organized and safe.
  5. Coach or assist a youth sports team. Rec leagues often need assistant coaches and referees.
  6. Library teen volunteer. Help with summer reading programs, events, or homework help.
  7. Little free library or pantry. Build one with permission, then keep it stocked.
  8. Help at a place of worship or community center. Meal programs, child care during events, setup crews.

Online and remote service ideas

  1. Citizen science. Classify images or data for research teams on platforms like Zooniverse.
  2. Transcribe historical documents. Projects like the Smithsonian Transcription Center and the Library of Congress "By the People" need volunteers.
  3. Proofread public-domain books with Distributed Proofreaders.
  4. Improve Wikipedia articles on local history or topics you know well.
  5. Tutor online through a program your school or library runs.
  6. Volunteer remotely for a student-run nonprofit. Writing, research, design and outreach can all be done from home.

Check each program's site for eligibility. For more, see our guide to online volunteer opportunities for students.

Skills-based service ideas

Skills-based service means you use something you're already good at, or want to get good at, to help an organization. It's the most valuable kind of help, because small groups rarely have these skills on staff.

  1. Design flyers or social graphics for a local nonprofit or school club.
  2. Build or update a simple website for a community group.
  3. Translate materials into a language spoken in your community, with a staff member reviewing them.
  4. Shoot and edit a short video that explains what an organization does.
  5. Research grants or sponsors for a small nonprofit and organize them in a spreadsheet.
  6. Teach a free class at a library or community center, such as basic coding or chess.
  7. Build something useful. For example, a STEM project like a solar study light made for a real recipient.

How to log and verify your hours

Hours you can't prove are hard to use. A few habits make verification simple.

  • Log every session the same day. Date, start and end time, organization, supervisor, and one sentence about what you did.
  • Get the supervisor's name and email on day one. You'll need it for signatures later.
  • Use your school's form or system. Some schools use paper forms, others use online platforms. Ask which one before you start.
  • Get signatures regularly. Monthly is a good rhythm. People forget details, and supervisors sometimes move on.
  • Keep copies. Save a photo or PDF of every signed form.

A sample log line: Oct 4, 9:00 to 12:00 (3 hrs), Eastside Food Pantry, supervisor A. Chen, sorted donations and packed 40 family boxes.

Honor society and graduation requirements

National Honor Society chapters, other honor societies, and some schools ask students to complete service hours. The exact rules vary by chapter and school, and your adviser or counselor has them.

Most requirements share the same shape. The service is unpaid and done for others, not for a family business or yourself. It's with a nonprofit, school or public agency. There's a deadline each semester or year, and a signature from an adult supervisor who isn't a relative. Many chapters also want leadership in service, like organizing a drive instead of just showing up to one. If you're thinking ahead, our guide to leadership experience in high school can help.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing something only because it's quick, then dreading every shift.
  • Counting hours that your school doesn't accept, like paid work or helping family.
  • Waiting until the deadline to ask for signatures.
  • Doing ten things once instead of one thing well. Depth is easier to describe on applications.

Turn service into real responsibility

Any idea on this list can be meaningful if you show up consistently. If you want service that also builds skills, gives you real projects, and comes with weekly feedback, a student-run nonprofit is a strong choice, because you'll be trusted with work the organization truly needs.

FAQ

Questions

How many service hours do I need?

It depends on your school, club or honor society chapter. Ask your counselor or chapter adviser for the current requirement.

Does remote volunteering count as community service?

Yes, as long as it's unpaid work for an organization that can verify it. Confirm with your school before you start.

Can Flash Forward sign my service hours?

Yes. Flash Forward signs off volunteer service hours for intern work, including through x2VOL for Bellevue School District students.

Is one long-term activity better than many short ones?

Yes. Steady work with one organization gives you more to show and more to talk about than many one-time events.
