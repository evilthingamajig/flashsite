Free summer programs for high school students: where to look and what to skip

Free summer programs for high school students usually come from universities, libraries, local government, school districts and nonprofits, not from glossy brochures. Start local, judge each option by what you'll actually do, and don't be afraid to build your own summer.

Want real experience now? Flash Forward's remote internship is open to high school and college students.

By the Flash Forward Foundation team · Updated October 12, 2026

Where to find free summer programs for high school students

Free summer programs for high school students exist, but they rarely advertise as loudly as expensive ones. Most are run by public institutions or nonprofits with a mission to serve young people, so you find them by asking around and searching locally. Start early, since popular options often fill up or close applications in winter or spring.

University outreach programs

Many colleges run outreach programs for local high schoolers, often through a department, a STEM center, or an office focused on access. Plenty are no-cost or offer aid. Search your nearest university's site for "high school," "outreach," or "pre-college," and look for programs run by the school itself, not a third party renting its campus.

Public libraries

Libraries are one of the most underused summer resources. Many offer teen programs, workshops, maker spaces, reading challenges and teen volunteer roles. Ask at the teen services desk what's planned for summer.

Local government and parks departments

City and county parks departments often hire or train teens as junior counselors, lifeguards or volunteers. Some cities run youth councils or summer youth employment programs. Check your city's website and call the recreation office if it's unclear.

Your school district

Districts sometimes run summer enrichment, credit recovery, or bridge programs, and some list outside opportunities on a counselor's page. Your counselor is the fastest way to find out. Ask specifically: "What summer options do students here usually do that cost little or nothing?"

Online courses

Many universities and nonprofits publish course material online that anyone can study. A self-paced course can teach you a real skill. Pair it with a project so you have something to show for it.

Volunteering

Summer is a good time to commit to one organization: a food bank, a hospital volunteer program, a shelter, a park, or a remote project. See our list of online volunteer opportunities for students if you want something you can do from home.

Internships

High school internships exist, especially at small organizations and nonprofits that need the help. Our guide on how to get an internship in high school walks through finding one and writing the email.

How to judge a program's quality

A program's name and price tell you little. What matters is what you'll do each day and what you'll leave with. Ask these questions before you apply:

  • What will I actually do? Look for specifics: lab work, a project, a performance, real tasks. "Exposure" and "networking" alone are thin.
  • Who runs it? A university department, library, public agency or established nonprofit is easier to trust than a company you can't trace.
  • Who teaches or supervises? Names and roles should be easy to find.
  • What will I have at the end? A finished project, a skill, a portfolio piece, or a reference from someone who watched you work.
  • What does it really cost? Check for travel, housing, materials and "activity fees," even in programs called free.

Red flags: pay-to-play "prestige" programs

Some summer programs charge high fees and lean on exclusive-sounding names, selective-sounding invitations, or a famous campus. A price tag isn't an accomplishment. Admissions readers care about what you did, not what you paid. Watch for these signs:

  • A "nomination" letter that arrives unprompted, followed by a request for payment.
  • Promises about college admissions or vague claims that the program "looks great" on applications.
  • A university's name in the title, but a separate company actually running it.
  • Little detail about daily activities, and a lot about the prestige.
  • Pressure to pay a deposit quickly before you can ask questions.

If something feels off, it probably is. Run it past a counselor before you hand over a deposit.

Build your own summer

You don't need a program to have a strong summer. A self-directed plan can be more interesting, and it shows initiative. Try combining three pieces:

  1. One commitment. A volunteer role, a job, or an internship you show up to every week.
  2. One thing to learn. An online course, a library workshop, or a book list with a goal.
  3. One thing to make. A project with a finished result: a website, a research write-up, a short film, a fundraiser, or a build. Our passion project ideas guide has starting points.

Write your plan down with weekly goals, and keep a short log of what you did. That log turns into resume lines and application material later. It also helps you notice if the summer is drifting.

Here's what a realistic plan might look like: three afternoons a week of remote nonprofit work, one library coding workshop, and a personal project building a small solar-powered device, with a two-page write-up due by August.

A remote internship that fits a summer

Flash Forward Foundation is a student-run 501(c)(3). Our interns build $5.17 solar study lights, and partner organizations in Haiti, India and DR Congo put them in the hands of students without electricity. About 655 million people lacked electricity in 2024 (Tracking SDG7: The Energy Progress Report 2026), so the need is real. Our internship is unpaid and fully remote, and it runs year-round, so it can fit into a summer as well as a school year.

You'll go through a short orientation and department course, then do at least two real projects in Marketing & Communications, Partnerships & Operations, or Fundraising & Development. Managers review your work weekly, and strong work can be published as a portfolio piece with your permission. Flash Forward also signs off volunteer service hours for intern work.

Make your summer about real work

The best summer isn't the most expensive one. It's the one where you take on real responsibility and come out with something you made. Joining a student-run nonprofit gives you that, because the work you do is work the organization needs.

If that sounds like your summer, apply to the Flash Forward internship. The application is a short Google Form that asks for your resume.

FAQ

Questions

Are there really free summer programs for high school students?

Yes, usually through universities, libraries, local government, school districts and nonprofits. Check each program's own site for current costs and eligibility.

Is a pricey summer program better for college applications?

No. Admissions readers care about what you did and learned, not what a program cost.

Can I do the Flash Forward internship over the summer?

Yes. It's an unpaid, fully remote internship that runs year-round, so it can fit into your summer or the school year.

When should I start looking for summer options?

Start in winter if you can. Many programs set their application windows months before summer begins.
