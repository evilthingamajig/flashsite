How to get leadership experience in high school

You get leadership experience in high school by taking responsibility for outcomes, not by collecting titles. Step up on one task in a club, start something small, or lead a real project in a student-run organization, then describe what you did and what changed.

Want real experience now? Flash Forward's remote internship is open to high school and college students.

By the Flash Forward Foundation team · Updated October 12, 2026

What leadership actually means

Many students think leadership experience in high school means being club president or team captain. Those roles count, but they're limited, and plenty of people hold them without doing much. What colleges, employers and internship programs actually look for is evidence that you noticed something that needed doing, took responsibility, worked with other people and got it done.

That means you can build real leadership experience without an official title. This guide covers how.

Leadership without a title

Leadership starts with small, unglamorous moves. Look for gaps: things that should happen but don't, or that happen badly.

  • Your club's meetings run long and nothing gets decided. Offer to write and share an agenda.
  • New members drop out after a month. Volunteer to welcome them and check in.
  • Your group project has no plan. Suggest a shared document with tasks and deadlines.
  • Your team keeps forgetting events. Set up a simple shared calendar or reminder system.

None of these need permission or a title. They do need follow-through. Doing one consistently for a semester is more meaningful than holding an office in name only.

How to step up in clubs

If you're already in a club, you're in a good position. Here's a practical way to grow your role:

  1. Be reliable first. Show up, finish what you say you'll do, and do it on time. Leaders get chosen from the people others can count on.
  2. Ask the current leaders what they need. Something like: "Is there one thing I could take off your plate this month?"
  3. Own one piece completely. Run one event, manage the club's social account or handle fundraising for one drive. Make it clearly yours.
  4. Bring others in. Ask a newer member to help you, and teach them how you do it. Developing other people is a core part of leadership.
  5. Run for a role, or propose a new one. If there's no position for what you're doing, suggest creating one, like "outreach coordinator" or "events lead."

Start something

Starting something new shows initiative in the clearest way. It doesn't have to be a big organization. It could be a study group, a tutoring program, a school-wide drive or a new club around an interest that isn't represented yet.

To make it work:

  • Start with a specific goal and a small group, even two or three people.
  • Find a teacher or adult sponsor if your school requires one.
  • Set a regular meeting time and a first concrete project.
  • Write down roles so everyone knows what they're responsible for.
  • Plan for what happens when you graduate, so it can continue without you.

Lead projects in student-run organizations

Student-run organizations, including student-run nonprofits, are one of the best places to practice leadership because students do the real work. There's no layer of adults handling the hard parts. If a partner email doesn't get sent or a deadline is missed, it matters.

In this kind of setting, leadership means owning a project from start to finish. For example:

  • Coordinate with an outside partner organization, keeping them updated and solving problems as they come up.
  • Plan the logistics of getting something from one place to another, tracking each step.
  • Build a shared resource, like a research database, that other team members rely on.
  • Set goals for a campaign, divide the work and report on results.

Each of these involves the core parts of leadership: planning, communicating, making decisions and being accountable for the result. Because the work is real, you'll also have specific outcomes to describe later.

Remote student-run organizations add another layer. Leading people over email, messages and video calls takes clear writing and careful follow-up, skills that transfer to almost any job. Learn more about how remote internships work.

How to describe leadership on applications

Whether you're writing a resume, a college activities list or an internship application, the rule is the same: describe what you did and what changed, not just your title.

Use a simple structure

For each leadership example, cover the situation, your action and the result. Compare:

  • Weak: "Vice president of Environmental Club."
  • Stronger: "Vice president, Environmental Club. Started a classroom recycling program in 8 rooms and trained 5 members to run weekly pickups."
  • Weak: "Helped organize events."
  • Stronger: "Planned and ran a fall tutoring night for 30 students, recruiting 6 volunteer tutors and coordinating room setup with staff."

Leadership without a title still goes on paper

If you didn't have an official title, describe the role you played: "Organized meeting agendas and tracked action items for a 20-member club." On a resume, you can list yourself as "Member" and let the bullet show the leadership. Our guide on how to put volunteer work on a resume has more bullet examples.

In essays and interviews

Pick one specific story rather than listing everything. Explain a problem you faced, a decision you made and what you learned, including what you'd do differently. Honest reflection is more convincing than a story where everything went perfectly.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Joining many clubs to collect titles without doing much in any of them.
  • Doing everything yourself instead of involving others.
  • Claiming credit for a team's work. Say "led a team of four" instead of implying you did it alone.
  • Waiting to be chosen instead of volunteering for responsibility.

FAQ

Questions

Do you need a title to show leadership?

No. Taking ownership of a task, organizing others or starting something new all count. Describe what you did and what changed.

Is it too late to get leadership experience as a junior or senior?

No. Owning one meaningful project for a few months can give you a strong example to describe.

What counts as leadership on a college application?

Any role where you took responsibility for an outcome and worked with others, in school, at home, at work or in your community.

Should I start my own club or join an existing one?

Join first if a club already covers your interest, and grow your role there. Start one when there's a clear gap no existing group fills.
