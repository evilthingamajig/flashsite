Grant writing for students: a beginner's guide

Grant writing for students comes down to explaining a real need, a clear plan and an honest budget to a funder whose goals match yours. Here's how each part works, with a short example.

Want real experience now? Flash Forward's remote internship is open to high school and college students.

By the Flash Forward Foundation team · Updated October 12, 2026

What a grant is

Grant writing for students starts with one idea: a grant is money one organization gives another to do specific work, and it usually doesn't have to be paid back. Foundations, companies and government agencies all give grants. In return, the group that receives the money agrees to do what it proposed and to report on how it went.

A grant proposal is the document that asks for that money. It explains a problem, a plan to address it, what the plan costs and how you'll know it worked. Most grants go to organizations, such as nonprofits and schools, rather than to individuals. As a student, you'll usually write one for a club, a school program or a nonprofit you work with.

How funders decide

Every funder has its own process, but most reviewers ask a similar set of questions:

  • Fit. Does this project match what we fund: the cause, the place and the type of organization?
  • Need. Is the problem real and clearly explained?
  • Plan. Are the activities specific and realistic?
  • Cost. Does the budget make sense for what's proposed?
  • Trust. Can this organization actually do it, and will it report back honestly?

Fit comes first. A well-written proposal sent to a funder that doesn't support your cause will almost always be turned down. That's why research matters as much as writing.

The usual parts of a proposal

Funders use different forms and section names, but most proposals include the parts below. If a funder gives its own order or word limits, follow those exactly.

Need statement

The problem, who it affects and why it matters. Use reliable, named sources and keep the focus on the people you serve, not on your organization.

Goals and objectives

Goals describe the big change you want. Objectives are specific, measurable steps toward it. "Help students study at night" is a goal. "Build and hand over a set number of lights to a partner by a set date" is an objective, because someone can check whether it happened.

Activities

What you'll actually do, who does it and when. A simple timeline by month or by phase is often enough.

Budget

What the money pays for, item by item. The numbers should match the activities. If the proposal says you'll build 100 of something, the budget should show the cost of 100.

Evaluation

How you'll measure whether the project worked: what you'll count, track or ask, and how you'll report it back to the funder.

Organization background

Who you are, your mission and anything that shows you can deliver, such as past projects, partners or the people on your team.

How to research funders

  1. Start with who already supports similar work. Nonprofits often thank their funders in annual reports and on their websites.
  2. Look at community foundations in your area, local businesses with giving programs and service clubs. Many fund local youth and education projects.
  3. Read each funder's guidelines closely: what they fund, where, roughly how much and who can apply.
  4. Keep a simple spreadsheet with the funder's name, focus, typical grant size, deadline, link and a note on fit.
  5. If the guidelines invite questions, send a short, polite email before applying. Ask something specific, such as whether student-led projects under a nonprofit are eligible, rather than "will you fund us?"

Always pull eligibility and deadlines from the funder's own site right before you apply. Guidelines change, and last year's rules are an easy way to waste a good proposal.

A worked example: a short need statement

Here's a need statement for a project that builds solar study lights, like the light Flash Forward interns build. It uses only facts that can be checked:

About 655 million people lacked electricity in 2024, according to Tracking SDG7: The Energy Progress Report 2026. Without electricity at home, studying after sunset is hard. Our student team builds solar study lights for $5.17 each, and partner organizations give the lights to students who don't have electricity. A grant of [amount] would cover the parts for [number] more lights for these students.

Why this works:

  • It opens with a sourced number, not an opinion.
  • It names who's affected: students without electricity.
  • It connects the problem directly to a solution and its cost.
  • It leaves the amount and number blank instead of guessing. Those come from the real budget. At $5.17 per light, for example, every $517 covers the parts for 100 lights, and the budget section should show that math.

Notice what it doesn't do. It doesn't invent statistics, use dramatic language or promise results nobody has measured. Reviewers trust a plain, accurate statement more than an emotional one.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the fit check. Sending the same proposal to every funder you find rarely works.
  • Ignoring instructions. Word limits, file formats and required attachments matter. Some reviewers set aside proposals that don't follow them.
  • Vague objectives. "Raise awareness" is hard to measure. Say what will happen, how much and by when.
  • A budget that doesn't match the plan. Every activity that costs money should appear in the budget, and nothing extra.
  • Writing about yourself instead of the need. Your team matters, but the people you serve come first.
  • Unsourced or inflated numbers. One wrong figure can make a reviewer doubt everything else.
  • Writing at the last minute. Leave time for someone else to read it and catch errors.

A good habit: before you submit, read the funder's guidelines one more time with your proposal open beside them, and check off each requirement.

FAQ

Questions

Can a high school student write a grant?

Yes. Students often draft proposals for clubs, schools or nonprofits, though the organization itself usually submits it and receives the money.

What is the most important part of a grant proposal?

Fit with the funder comes first. After that, a clear need statement and a budget that matches the plan carry the most weight.

How long is a grant proposal?

It depends entirely on the funder. Some ask for a one-page letter, others for a multi-section application, so always check the guidelines.

How do I practice grant writing without a real funder?

Pick a real funder's published guidelines and draft a proposal for a club or cause you know, then ask a teacher or mentor to review it.
